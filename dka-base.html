Polydipsia

Excessive or abnormally increased thirst and fluid intake.

A symptom rather than a diagnosis; there is no single clinically useful numerical cut-off for ordinary bedside use.

Polyuria

Production of an abnormally large volume of urine — not simply urinary frequency.

In children, commonly defined around >2 L/m²/day; approximately >4 mL/kg/hour is a useful practical threshold.

🎧

Commentator

Clinical reasoning · 01
Okay, here we have Maya, a 12-year-old girl presenting to A&E. For about three weeks she has been unusually thirsty and drinking considerably more than normal. We call excessive thirst polydipsia — abnormally increased thirst and fluid intake. Maya is also passing large amounts of urine. That's polyuria: increased urine volume, not simply urinary frequency. In children, a useful definition is more than about 2 L/m²/day, or roughly >4 mL/kg/hour. So we have polyuria and polydipsia together. Now look at the next clue: four kilograms of weight loss in three weeks. That's a lot. Where has that weight gone?
THINK 01

What is most likely contributing substantially to Maya's rapid fall in weight?

Correct: significant renal water loss can produce rapid measurable weight loss. But keep a second idea in reserve — in insulin deficiency, catabolism of fat and muscle also contributes. We will come back to it.

Commentator

Timeline before label
Vomiting contributes, but the timeline matters. Polyuria, polydipsia and weight loss have been developing for three weeks; vomiting is much more recent, and no diarrhoea is described. So gastrointestinal loss alone cannot explain the whole picture. The better question is: why are her kidneys losing so much water?
THINK 02

Which explanation best fits the first three weeks?

Yes. The case points first to renal water loss. Vomiting may worsen dehydration later, but it does not explain the preceding three-week history.
03
A respiratory clue

Why so deep?

RR 33/min matters. But the pattern matters more: Maya's breaths are unusually deep despite an SpO₂ of 99%.

THINK 03

What is the best explanation for deep, rapid breathing in this context?

Correct. In metabolic acidosis the respiratory system lowers PaCO₂ by increasing alveolar ventilation. Deep laboured respiration in severe acidosis is classically called Kussmaul breathing.
🎧

Commentator

From breathing to metabolism
Maya is not just tachypnoeic — her breaths are deep. With SpO₂ 99%, ask whether the lungs are responding to a problem elsewhere. Metabolic acidosis increases ventilation to lower CO₂. This is Kussmaul breathing. Most medically trained learners will recognise DKA now. So instead of dragging out the diagnosis, we move to: why does insulin deficiency produce all of this?

Commentator

Normal physiology
Insulin is more than a “glucose-lowering hormone”. It is an energy-available signal: use glucose, store energy, and suppress breakdown of fat. Remove that signal and metabolism swings towards a fasting/starvation pattern despite abundant circulating glucose.
THINK 04

If the insulin signal disappears, what does the metabolic system behave as though is happening?

Exactly. The key conceptual shift is towards catabolism, amplified by counter-regulatory hormones.

Commentator

Build ketoacidosis
Loss of insulin removes the brake on lipolysis. Free fatty acids reach the liver and ketone production accelerates. β-hydroxybutyrate and acetoacetate accumulate; bicarbonate is consumed buffering the acid load and pH falls. Now weight loss, abdominal symptoms and deep breathing belong to the same physiological story.
THINK 06

How can the lungs partially compensate for this metabolic acidosis?

Correct. Increased alveolar ventilation lowers PaCO₂. It compensates; it does not remove the underlying ketoacid production.

What is most likely true of Maya's total-body potassium?

Exactly. Osmotic diuresis causes urinary potassium loss, while insulin deficiency and hyperosmolality favour extracellular redistribution. The serum concentration can look reassuring while total-body stores are depleted.

Commentator

Compartment thinking
Serum K⁺ is a concentration in one compartment. Total-body stores may be depleted through urine while extracellular redistribution keeps the initial serum value normal or high. Treatment can then reveal that deficit quickly.

THINK 08

Glucose is approaching the target range, but ketones remain elevated. Should insulin simply be stopped?

Correct. DKA is not simply “high glucose”. Insulin continues according to the paediatric protocol until ketoacidosis resolves; glucose-containing fluids can be used when needed to permit ongoing insulin safely.

Commentator

Management from mechanism
Fluid addresses volume deficit. Insulin switches off ketogenesis. Electrolytes must be followed as losses and redistribution evolve. Treat the ketoacidosis, not merely the glucose number.

THINK 09

What complication must be considered immediately?

Yes. New headache, altered behaviour/consciousness, bradycardia or hypertension during paediatric DKA are neurological red flags requiring immediate senior assessment and emergency treatment according to the current paediatric protocol.
🎧

Commentator

Complication + misconception
Neurological deterioration in DKA is an emergency. The older “too much fluid = cerebral oedema” story is too simplistic; DKA-related cerebral injury is multifactorial. The bedside priority is early recognition, escalation and guideline-based emergency treatment.

No multiple choice now

Explain it yourself

Final challenge

Explain Maya's illness to a medical student.

Start with insulin deficiency. Explain polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss and deep breathing without using “because DKA does that”.

Core chain:
Insulin deficiency → reduced glucose utilisation + increased hepatic glucose output → hyperglycaemia → glycosuria → osmotic diuresis → polyuria → dehydration → polydipsia.

In parallel: insulin deficiency + counter-regulatory hormones → lipolysis → free fatty acids → hepatic ketogenesis → ketoacid accumulation → bicarbonate consumption + falling pH → compensatory hyperventilation / Kussmaul breathing.

Weight loss comes from both fluid loss and catabolism. Potassium illustrates the compartment problem: total-body depletion can coexist with an initially normal/high serum concentration.
