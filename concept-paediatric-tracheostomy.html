TRACHEOSTOMYTHINK BEFORE YOU BAG

A tracheostomy emergency becomes much easier once you stop seeing “a tube in the neck” and start seeing two possible routes to the lungs. Work out which route exists, decide whether the tube is patent and in the trachea, then preserve oxygenation while you fix the problem.

The entire lesson is built around one question: where can air still get to the lungs?
01 · Build the mental model

A tracheostomy creates a second entrance to the airway

Normally air travels nose or mouth → pharynx → larynx → trachea → lungs. A tracheostomy bypasses the upper airway and enters the trachea directly through the neck.

Route A

Upper airway

Nose / mouth → larynx → trachea. This route may still be usable, partly usable, or completely unavailable.

Route B

Stoma / tracheostomy

Neck → trachea → lungs. This route depends on the tube being in the trachea and patent.

Why this matters: every emergency decision depends on which route can still deliver oxygen. A child with an intact upper airway gives you a rescue route that a child with an obstructed or surgically excluded upper airway does not have.

Before anything goes wrong, what is the single most useful fact to know?

Exactly. Tube size and equipment matter, but in a crisis the upper-airway question tells you whether face-mask ventilation or oral intubation can rescue oxygenation.
Why start here? The narration builds the two-route model before introducing any algorithm.
02 · Fresh versus established tracheostomy

Why is the first week different?

A fresh tracheostomy is not yet a mature tunnel. There is a skin opening and a tracheal opening, and early on those openings can become misaligned if the tube is removed.

Skin

Opening in the neck

The external opening can move relative to the trachea.

→
Trachea

Opening into the airway

If the two openings do not line up, a replacement tube can enter soft tissue instead of the trachea.

Maturation sutures

They bring the trachea towards the skin and help keep the two openings aligned while the tract matures.

Stay sutures

They are a rescue aid. If an early change is unavoidable, they can be lifted to help bring the trachea forwards and open the tracheal incision.

The false-passage problem: if a replacement tube is pushed into soft tissue rather than the tracheal lumen, ventilation can force air into the neck and chest tissues instead of the lungs.

A day-2 tracheostomy is completely blocked. The child is cyanosed and cannot breathe. Which principle wins?

Correct. Early tube change is higher risk, not absolutely forbidden. If a completely blocked tube is the reason the child cannot breathe, leaving it in place is also dangerous.
The key distinction: “avoid changing” is a risk-management rule; it is not a reason to leave an obstructed airway in place.
03 · The first 24–36 hours

What are you actively watching for?

The early post-operative period is where fresh-tracheostomy complications are most likely to declare themselves. Use the child, not just the monitor or a single X-ray.

Tube problem

Obstruction or displacement: increased work of breathing, desaturation, inability to pass the suction catheter, absent/poor airflow.

Pneumothorax

Acute respiratory deterioration, asymmetrical or reduced air entry, difficulty ventilating.

Surgical emphysema

Swelling of neck/face/upper chest and palpable crepitus. Tapes may suddenly feel tighter as the neck swells.

Bleeding

Some blood-stained secretions can follow surgery. Ongoing or significant bleeding is different and needs escalation.

Tube position

Post-operative imaging may confirm tube position above the carina, but clinical assessment remains continuous.

Your best instruments

Eyes, ears and hands: look at the child, listen for airflow and breath sounds, feel chest movement and the neck.

Why not rely on SpO₂? Oxygen saturation can be a late signal. Supervision and direct observation let you identify obstruction or displacement before the child has exhausted their reserve.
Clinical focus: convert a list of complications into visible bedside signals.
04 · Prevention

The safest emergency is the one you prevent

Small paediatric tubes obstruct quickly. A tracheostomy also bypasses the nose, so the child loses normal warming, humidification and filtration of inspired gas.

1
Humidify

Dry gas → dry secretions → thicker mucus → narrower effective lumen → obstruction.

2
Hydrate and review drying factors

Illness, fluid restriction, oxygen and drying medications can all make secretions more difficult to clear.

3
Suction to the prescribed tube length

Go far enough to clear the tube, but not repeatedly beyond its tip. Distal trauma can cause bleeding and granulation.

4
Secure the airway

Check tape tension and tube position. A mobile child can dislodge a poorly secured tube.

5
Keep the emergency equipment with the child

Knowledge cannot rescue an airway if the correct replacement tubes and equipment are elsewhere.

6
Supervise

See and hear the child. Early recognition turns a catastrophe into a manageable airway problem.

Mechanism first: each preventive action is linked to the complication it prevents.
05 · Emergency algorithm

The child suddenly deteriorates. What do you do first?

Do not jump straight to bagging the tracheostomy. First establish whether the tube is actually in the neck and whether it is patent.

A
Call for help and give oxygen

In a major event, get emergency support moving early. Oxygen can be offered to both potential airway routes while you assess.

B
Look: is the tube in the stoma?

Lift the dressing if necessary. A displaced tube can look deceptively normal from above.

C
Pass the suction catheter

This is both a treatment and a diagnostic test: can you pass through the full tube length?

D
If it is in situ and patent, move on

Do not become trapped by the tracheostomy. Assess breathing and circulation like any other deteriorating child.

The catheter passes easily to the expected distance. Airflow is present. The child remains shocked and tachycardic. What next?

Exactly. A tracheostomy is common enough to attract attention, but it is not the cause of every deterioration.
Core habit: in situ? patent? If yes, move on.
06 · The catheter will not pass

Is this a partial obstruction—or a complete one?

Partial obstruction

The child is still moving air

Try to clear the tube: suction, prescribed nebulisation/humidification and reassessment. In a fresh tracheostomy, preserving a functioning tube may be safer than an unnecessary early change.

Complete obstruction

The child cannot ventilate

Call for emergency help. If the tube is the obstruction and cannot be cleared, it has to come out / be changed even if fresh.

A child is blue, apnoeic and the catheter cannot pass. The tube is visibly in the stoma. What is the dangerous mistake?

Correct. Positive-pressure ventilation through an obstructed or displaced tube does not guarantee ventilation of the lungs and can worsen air leak into tissues if the tube is outside the trachea.
Fresh tracheostomy nuance: if the child is stable enough, spend a little more effort clearing a partially blocked fresh tube because replacement carries tract risk. If the child cannot breathe, oxygenation takes priority.
Decision threshold: partial obstruction can sometimes be cleared; complete obstruction in a crashing child cannot be watched.
07 · The tube is out

Now the upper-airway question becomes everything

Usable upper airway

You still have Route A

If the tracheostomy cannot be replaced, oxygenate via the face. If bag-mask ventilation is needed, occluding the stoma can reduce escape of gas through the neck. Advanced help may use an oral airway strategy if appropriate.

No usable upper airway

The stoma is the only route

Replacement via the stoma is critical. If a tube cannot be inserted, emergency oxygenation/ventilation may need to be delivered at the stoma while expert airway help arrives.

Do not force a fresh tract. If an early replacement will not pass and you are successfully oxygenating the child by another route, keep oxygenating and wait for expert help rather than repeatedly creating trauma.

A fresh tube has come out. You cannot reinsert it. The child has a known patent upper airway and is oxygenating well with face-mask support. What next?

Yes. Oxygenation is the goal. Repeated attempts through a fresh tract can create a false passage and make the situation worse.
Rescue logic: use whatever airway route actually reaches the lungs.
08 · Surgical emphysema

Why does the neck suddenly feel like bubble wrap?

Surgical emphysema means air has escaped from the airway into soft tissues. The important question is not the word “emphysema”; it is where is the pressurised air going?

1
There is a route for air out of the trachea

This can occur around a fresh stoma or when a tube is displaced / in a false passage.

2
Positive pressure drives air into tissue planes

Air tracks through neck and chest soft tissue rather than entering the lungs normally.

3
The child swells and crepitus appears

Neck, face or upper chest can enlarge. Tracheostomy tapes may suddenly feel tighter.

Critical link: new/worsening surgical emphysema during bagging should make you question whether the tube is truly intratracheal. Continuing high-pressure ventilation through a false passage can rapidly worsen the air leak.

A fresh tracheostomy suddenly develops increasing neck swelling while you bag through the tube. What is the most important interpretation?

Correct. The swelling is evidence that pressurised gas is escaping into tissues. Stop assuming that bag movement equals lung ventilation.
The “why”: surgical emphysema becomes memorable once learners picture gas tracking along tissue planes.
09 · Pneumothorax and bleeding

Two complications where the tracheostomy may be only part of the story

Pneumothorax

Think beyond the tube if respiratory distress persists despite a patent airway. Look for asymmetry, reduced air entry and difficulty ventilating. Significant respiratory compromise is an emergency.

Bleeding

Small blood staining can follow a new surgical airway. Ongoing or significant bleeding is different: call for expert help, assess severity, and use direct pressure to an external bleeding site when appropriate.

Why this matters: a patent tracheostomy does not exclude a chest problem. Once the tube is shown to be patent and intratracheal, keep moving through ABC rather than repeatedly manipulating the airway.

The suction catheter passes, the tube is clearly in situ, but the child is acutely hypoxic with markedly reduced air entry on one side. What should move up your differential?

Correct. A tracheostomy can distract you from the rest of the respiratory system.
Diagnostic discipline: prove the airway problem before treating every collapse as a tube problem.
10 · Know the tube

You cannot manage an emergency if you do not know what is in the neck

Tube size & length

Know the exact device and suction depth. Paediatric/neonatal variants may have the same diameter but different lengths.

Cuffed or uncuffed?

A cuff changes removal and ventilation. Before removing a conventional inflated cuffed tube, the cuff must be deflated using the correct method for that cuff system.

Specialist tubes

Some flexible wire-reinforced tubes have MRI restrictions. Tube-specific emergency information belongs on the bedside chart.

Bedside habit: at the start of a shift, know the indication for the tracheostomy, whether there is a usable upper airway, the tube type/size/length, cuff status, prescribed suction depth, and what replacement tubes are in the emergency box.
Practical focus: the tube chart is not paperwork; it is airway information.
11 · Final challenge

Explain the patient from first principles

Scenario

Day 2 after tracheostomy

A 6-year-old suddenly becomes distressed. SpO₂ falls. The suction catheter stops halfway down the tube. You can see the tracheostomy flange at the neck. You attempt gentle suction but cannot clear it. The child becomes cyanosed. The notes state: upper airway patent.

1. What is your working airway diagnosis?

The catheter cannot traverse the tube and the child is failing to ventilate. Treat this as critical obstruction while calling for emergency help.

2. Why is removing the tube risky—but still potentially necessary?

Exactly. This is the central tension of a fresh tracheostomy emergency.

3. The tube is removed and cannot be replaced. What rescue option do you have that a child with no upper airway would not?

Correct. This is why “upper airway patent?” belongs at the very top of the mental model.

4. During an attempted reinsertion the neck rapidly swells with crepitus. What has probably happened?

Yes. Stop assuming the tracheostomy route is ventilating the lungs; preserve oxygenation by the working route and escalate.
Final task: learners should be able to derive the correct action rather than memorise a disconnected algorithm.
12 · Bring it together

The whole lesson in six questions

1 · Why is there a tracheostomy?Know the anatomy and whether the upper airway is usable.
2 · Is the tube actually in the trachea?Look at the stoma; do not assume from the flange alone.
3 · Is it patent?Pass the suction catheter to the prescribed tube length.
4 · Fresh or established?Fresh means tract-risk and false-passage risk—not “never remove”.
5 · Which route can oxygenate?Upper airway, stoma, or both. Use the route that reaches the lungs.
6 · If the tube is fine, what else is wrong?Move through breathing and circulation; consider pneumothorax, bleeding and non-airway causes.
MeducateMe rule: do not memorise “suction → change → bag” as a ritual. Understand the anatomy, prove where the air is going, and preserve oxygenation while expert help is mobilised.
