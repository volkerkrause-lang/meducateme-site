05 · Where can sodium be lost?

Follow the fluid out

GI / skin / drains

Vomiting · diarrhoea · ileostomy · NG aspiration · burns · sweating · fistulae · biliary/pancreatic/pleural/abdominal drainage.

Renal sodium loss

Diuretics · tubular disorders · adrenal insufficiency · cerebral salt wasting.

06 · Dilutional hyponatraemia

Low sodium without losing sodium

Normal response to dilute plasma

ADH should switch off → dilute urine → free-water excretion.

If urine stays concentrated

ADH is active

Then ask why.

07 · SIADH

ADH active when osmotically it should not be

Water retained

Small ECF expansion suppresses renin/aldosterone and increases sodium excretion, preventing massive oedema.

Think of

Pulmonary disease · CNS disease · pain · nausea · postoperative states · medication.

Exclude important mimics: adrenal insufficiency and severe hypothyroidism.

08 · Osmolality

Is it true hypotonic hyponatraemia?

Hypotonic

Low Na + low plasma osmolality. True dilute extracellular fluid.

Hypertonic

Severe hyperglycaemia draws water into ECF and dilutes sodium.

Pseudo

Very high lipid/protein can interfere with some laboratory methods.

09 · Urine osmolality

Ask the kidney whether ADH is on

<100

Very dilute urine → ADH largely suppressed.

Think excess water intake or very low solute intake.

concentrated

ADH is active → now ask why.

10 · Volume states

Hypovolaemic · euvolaemic · hypervolaemic

Hypovolaemic

Sodium + water lost → circulation falls → ADH rises.

Euvolaemic

Classic SIADH pattern.

Hypervolaemic

Total fluid excess but effective arterial underfilling keeps ADH active.

11 · Urinary sodium

Is the kidney conserving sodium or wasting it?

Low urine sodium

Appropriate sodium conservation → think extrarenal losses such as vomiting, diarrhoea, stoma or skin losses.

High urine sodium despite hypovolaemia

Think renal sodium loss: diuretics, adrenal insufficiency, tubular disorders, cerebral salt wasting.

12 · SIADH vs cerebral salt wasting

Same labs. Different physiology.

SIADH

Primary water retention · roughly euvolaemic.

Cerebral salt wasting

Primary renal sodium loss · hypovolaemic · high urine output · falling weight · negative fluid balance.

Meningitis: Na 124, Uosm 520, urine Na 75. Can you diagnose SIADH?

13 · The brain

Why symptoms become neurological

brain cell

Water enters brain cells

Headache · nausea · vomiting · confusion · drowsiness · seizures · coma.

Speed matters: acute falls are more dangerous because adaptation has not yet occurred.

14 · Acute vs chronic

Two opposite dangers

Too rapid a fall

Cerebral oedema.

Too rapid correction of chronic hyponatraemia

Adapted brain cells lose water rapidly → osmotic demyelination syndrome.

15 · Emergency

What if the child is seizing?

Treat the brain first

Severe symptomatic hypotonic hyponatraemia requires urgent hypertonic saline according to the local paediatric emergency protocol. The initial goal is enough sodium rise to reverse dangerous neurological symptoms — not normalization to 140.

16 · Treat the mechanism

There is no single treatment for “Na 122”

Hypovolaemia

Restore circulating volume and treat the source.

SIADH

Remove trigger where possible and restrict free water.

Adrenal insufficiency

Glucocorticoid replacement + appropriate fluid/electrolyte management.

Cerebral salt wasting

Replace sodium and volume.

17 · Practical sequence

A pathway you can use

1. Is the child severely symptomatic?
2. Is this true hypotonic hyponatraemia?
3. What is the urine osmolality?
4. Why is ADH active?
5. What is the kidney doing with sodium?
6. What is the underlying cause?

19 · Reconstruction

Hyponatraemia understood as physiology

Serum sodium is a concentration, not total body sodium.
Hyponatraemia can be sodium loss, water gain, or both.
Aldosterone retains sodium; ADH retains water.
Hypotonic plasma should produce dilute urine. Concentrated urine means ADH is active — ask why.
Volume state + urinary sodium help locate the mechanism.
Never treat the number in isolation. Treat the physiology that produced it.
