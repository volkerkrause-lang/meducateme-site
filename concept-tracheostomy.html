PAEDIATRIC
TRACHEOSTOMY

Do not memorise an emergency algorithm in isolation. First understand what the tracheostomy has changed anatomically, whether the child still has a usable upper airway, and why a fresh tract behaves differently from an established one. Then the emergency decisions become logical.

Narration script prepared for Chatterbox. Audio files are intentionally not generated yet while this remains a draft.

The question that changes everything
Can air still travel from mouth/nose to lungs?
Or is the tracheostomy the only functional airway?

That single distinction determines what rescue options remain if the tube blocks or comes out.

01 · Start with the anatomy

What has a tracheostomy actually changed?

A tracheostomy creates a new route into the trachea below the larynx. The child may still have a patent upper airway — or may not. Before any emergency, you need to know which situation you are dealing with.

Upper airway still usable

If the tracheostomy fails, oxygenation may still be possible through the mouth and nose. That can buy time.

No usable upper airway

The tracheostomy may be the only route to the lungs. Decannulation or complete obstruction can therefore remove the child's airway entirely.

Prediction

A child with a tracheostomy suddenly deteriorates. What information do you want immediately?

Why this matters: emergency management is not just “tracheostomy management”. It is airway management, and the available routes depend on the child's anatomy.
02 · Fresh versus established

Why is a new tracheostomy different?

In the first days, the skin opening and tracheal opening have not yet matured into a stable tract. If the tube is removed, those openings may no longer line up perfectly.

→
immature tissue path
→
tracheal opening

Maturation sutures

They help keep skin and tracheal openings aligned, making the tract safer.

Stay sutures

They can help bring the trachea forward during an emergency early change.

Prediction

Why avoid an unnecessary tube change in the first week?

The principle: an early change is higher risk — not forbidden. If a completely blocked tube is preventing ventilation, restoring oxygenation takes priority.
03 · Prevention

Why do paediatric tracheostomy tubes block?

The tracheostomy bypasses the nose and upper airway's normal warming, humidifying and filtering functions. A small tube plus dry secretions is an unforgiving combination.

drying / dehydration
→
thicker secretions
→
smaller effective lumen
→
obstruction

Humidity

Humidification replaces part of what the upper airway normally does and reduces secretion thickening.

Suction distance

Suction should stay within the tube length. Deep traumatic suction can damage tracheal tissue and promote bleeding or granulation.

Hydration

Post-operative fluid restriction, oxygen and drying drugs can all make secretions harder to clear.

Secure the airway

Tapes are not cosmetic. They are what keep the tube in the trachea of a moving child.

Why prevention matters: obstruction is dangerous, and a tube that has to be removed urgently may be difficult to replace — especially in a fresh tract.
04 · The early post-op red flags

What can go wrong after a fresh tracheostomy?

Tube obstruction

Secretions can rapidly occlude a small paediatric tube.

Tube displacement

The tube may sit partly or completely outside the tracheal lumen.

Surgical emphysema

Air escapes into soft tissues. Look for increasing neck/facial swelling, crepitus and tightening tapes.

Pneumothorax

Respiratory deterioration, asymmetrical air entry or difficulty ventilating should trigger urgent assessment.

Bleeding

Some blood-staining may occur after surgery, but significant or increasing bleeding is a red flag.

And remember...

Not every deterioration is caused by the tracheostomy. Once the tube is in situ and patent, continue ABC assessment.

05 · Emergency reasoning

The collapsing child: what are you trying to answer?

Is the tube in the neck?
→
Can a suction catheter pass?
→
Can the child be oxygenated?

1. Call for help + oxygen

Get the emergency response moving while you assess the airway.

2. Check in situ

Do not ventilate blindly through a tube that may no longer be in the trachea.

3. Suction

If the catheter passes, the tube is likely patent enough to move on. If it will not pass and the child cannot breathe, obstruction becomes much more likely.

4. Decide whether the tube must come out

A completely blocked tube that is preventing oxygenation cannot be left in place simply because the tract is fresh.

Core idea: the goal is oxygenation, not successful tracheostomy replacement at all costs.
06 · When the tube cannot be replaced

Do not create a false passage chasing the tube

In a fresh tract, forceful repeated reinsertion can drive the tube into soft tissue. Positive-pressure ventilation through a false passage can rapidly worsen surgical emphysema.

07 · Surgical emphysema

What does surgical emphysema actually mean?

Air has escaped from the airway into surrounding soft tissue. After a new tracheostomy, that can happen from air leaking around the tract, tissue injury, displacement or ventilation through a tube that is not correctly inside the trachea.

airway leak / malposition
→
air enters soft tissue
→
swelling + crepitus

Why is positive-pressure ventilation dangerous if the tube is displaced?

Practical clue: rapidly tightening neck tapes can be an early sign of increasing swelling even before it looks dramatic.
08 · Tube types

Know the tube in the neck

Emergency management changes with tube design. Size, length, cuff type, flexibility and MRI compatibility all matter. The learner should not treat every paediatric tracheostomy tube as interchangeable.

Uncuffed tubes

Common in children and designed to minimise long-term pressure on the tracheal wall where possible.

Cuffed tubes

Used when tighter ventilation or temporary airway protection is needed. Know how that specific cuff is inflated and deflated.

Flexible wire-reinforced tubes

Useful in selected anatomy, but some are not MRI compatible and may require planned tube change before scanning.

Emergency spare

The correct same-size tube and an appropriately smaller alternative must be immediately available according to local protocol.

Why tube knowledge matters: a rescue algorithm only works if you understand the hardware you are trying to remove, replace or ventilate through.
09 · Final challenge

Explain the patient

A child is 48 hours post-tracheostomy. The neck looks more swollen, the tapes suddenly feel tight, oxygen saturations are falling and the suction catheter will only pass a short distance. What mechanism could connect all four findings?

Reconstruct the logic: fresh tract → tube vulnerability → impaired airflow → positive pressure or leak into tissue → surgical emphysema → increasing swelling/tape tension. The emergency response is driven by oxygenation and airway patency, not by preserving the tracheostomy tube at all costs.
