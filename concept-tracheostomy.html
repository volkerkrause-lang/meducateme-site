PAEDIATRIC
TRACHEOSTOMY

A tracheostomy changes the route into the lungs, but the useful clinical question is not simply “does this child have a tracheostomy?” It is: what routes for oxygenation are available, and is the tube still connecting us to the trachea? Build that mental model first and the emergencies become easier to reason through.

Route 1
Mouth/nose → larynx → trachea → lungs
Route 2
Stoma → tube → trachea → lungs
01 · Anatomy first

What has a tracheostomy changed — and what has it not changed?

The tube enters the trachea through the anterior neck below the larynx, creating a shortcut around the upper airway. A standard tracheostomy does not itself disconnect the mouth and nose from the lungs. Whether that upper route is clinically usable depends on the child's underlying anatomy and the reason the tracheostomy was needed.

When the upper airway remains usable

If air can still pass from mouth and nose through the larynx into the trachea, oxygenation and ventilation from above may remain rescue options if the tracheostomy route fails.

When the upper airway is not usable

Severe upper-airway obstruction or other pathology may make the stoma the only effective route to the lungs. Loss of the tracheostomy route is therefore immediately more dangerous.

Prediction

A child with a tracheostomy suddenly deteriorates. Which piece of background information fundamentally changes your rescue options?

Clinical model: know the indication for the tracheostomy and whether the child can be oxygenated from above before an emergency occurs.
02 · Fresh versus established

Why is replacing a fresh tracheostomy more dangerous than replacing an established one?

Immediately after surgery there is a skin opening and a separate opening in the anterior tracheal wall, with freshly dissected tissue between them. Over time a more defined tract forms. Early on, removing the tube can allow tissue planes to shift, so a replacement tube may no longer naturally follow the path into the tracheal lumen.

skin stoma→fresh tissue plane→tracheal opening→tracheal lumen

What is a false passage?

The replacement tube enters soft tissue beside the trachea rather than the tracheal lumen. It can look as though the tube has gone into the neck while still failing to provide an airway.

What do the surgical sutures help with?

Stay or maturation sutures can help identify, stabilise or bring the trachea forward in the early postoperative period. Their exact arrangement depends on the surgical technique.

Why not simply leave a completely blocked fresh tube in place?

03 · Humidification and obstruction

Why does bypassing the upper airway make a small paediatric tube vulnerable to blockage?

The nose and upper airway normally warm and humidify inspired gas and help condition secretions. A tracheostomy bypasses much of this. Secretions can become thicker and more tenacious, and in a small paediatric tube even a modest deposit occupies a meaningful proportion of the lumen.

less conditioning→drier secretions→adherent plug→narrower lumen→obstruction

How does humidification help?

It replaces some of the conditioning normally provided by the upper airway and helps keep secretions easier to mobilise.

Why does hydration matter?

Systemic dehydration can make secretions more tenacious, adding to local drying from bypassing the upper airway.

Why suction only to the prescribed tube length?

The aim is to clear the tube without repeatedly traumatising tracheal mucosa beyond its tip. Deep suction can contribute to injury, bleeding and granulation.

Why are secure tapes clinically important?

They stabilise the tube. Movement or loosening can convert a functioning airway into partial or complete displacement.

04 · Early complications

What problems should make you worry after a new tracheostomy?

How does obstruction present?

Increasing work of breathing, reduced airflow, difficulty passing a suction catheter, desaturation or inability to ventilate can indicate a narrowed or blocked tube.

What does displacement mean?

The tube may be partly or completely outside the tracheal lumen. Partial displacement can be deceptive because the tube still appears to sit in the neck.

What does surgical emphysema tell you?

Air is entering soft tissue. Increasing neck or facial swelling, palpable crepitus and tapes becoming unexpectedly tight should prompt reassessment of airway and tube position.

When should pneumothorax enter your thinking?

Unexpected respiratory deterioration, asymmetric air entry or difficulty ventilating after airway surgery should broaden the differential beyond the tube itself.

How should bleeding be interpreted?

Minor postoperative blood staining and significant or increasing haemorrhage are not equivalent. Ongoing or substantial bleeding is a red flag.

Could the problem be somewhere else?

Yes. Once there is evidence that the tracheostomy is in position and patent, continue the full ABC assessment rather than repeatedly manipulating a functioning tube.

05 · The deteriorating child

How do you work out whether the tracheostomy itself is causing the emergency?

Ask what each observation tells you instead of treating the emergency sequence as a list to memorise.

Is the tube still where it should be?

Look at the neck, tube and fixation. Avoid blindly delivering positive pressure through a tube that may no longer sit correctly in the trachea.

Can a suction catheter pass?

The catheter is both treatment and diagnostic clue. Failure to pass it through the expected tube length raises concern for obstruction, kinking or displacement.

Is oxygen getting into the child?

Call for help and provide oxygen while assessing. The immediate physiological target is oxygenation, not preservation of the existing tube at all costs.

If the tube is the problem, does it need to come out?

A completely obstructed tube can itself prevent ventilation. A fresh tract makes removal consequential, but it does not make an unusable tube useful.

Reasoning sequence: position → patency → oxygenation → safest available airway route.
06 · Failed replacement

If the tube comes out and will not go back in, where should you ventilate?

This is where the anatomy from the start of the lesson becomes practical. Successful reinsertion is not the only acceptable outcome; effective oxygenation is the goal.

Upper airway usable

Oxygenate the face and stoma initially. If the tube is out and face-mask ventilation works, you have a functioning route to the lungs. Maintain oxygenation and obtain expert help rather than repeatedly forcing a tube through a fresh tract.

Upper airway not usable

The stoma becomes the rescue route. Oxygen and ventilation need to be directed through the stoma using an appropriate interface while expert help arrives.

Why can repeated reinsertion attempts make things worse?

A tube entering a false passage can damage tissue. If positive pressure is then delivered through it, gas can dissect through the neck instead of entering the trachea.

07 · Surgical emphysema

What exactly is surgical emphysema, and why can positive pressure make it worse?

Surgical emphysema is air within the subcutaneous soft tissues. It tells you that gas has escaped from the intended airway pathway; it does not by itself identify the exact site of the leak.

How can the air escape?

Mechanisms around a fresh tracheostomy include leakage around the new tract, tissue injury, tube displacement or ventilation through a tube that is not correctly within the tracheal lumen.

Why does positive pressure matter?

If the tube is malpositioned, each delivered breath may force more gas into surrounding tissue. Swelling can progress while effective lung ventilation worsens.

Bedside clue: tapes that suddenly become tight can be a mechanical clue to expanding neck swelling.
08 · Know the hardware

Why does the type of tracheostomy tube matter clinically?

“Tracheostomy tube” is not one device. Diameter, length, cuff, flexibility and construction affect routine care and emergency management. Know the actual tube in the child.

What changes with an uncuffed tube?

Air can pass around the tube as well as through it when the upper airway is patent. A cuff seal is not present.

What changes with a cuffed tube?

A cuff can provide a tighter seal when clinically required. In an emergency, know whether a cuff is present and how that specific device is managed.

Why do flexible or reinforced designs matter?

They may be selected for particular anatomy or positioning needs, and device construction can matter for imaging compatibility.

Why keep emergency replacement tubes nearby?

The bedside emergency equipment should include an appropriate replacement strategy, commonly including the same size and a smaller tube according to local practice.

09 · Bring it together

Can you explain the whole patient from one mechanism?

48 hours after tracheostomy

The child's saturations fall. The neck is becoming swollen, the tapes suddenly feel tighter, and the suction catheter only passes a short distance. What explanation links these observations?

fresh tract→obstruction / displacement→poor airflow to lungs+air into soft tissue→swelling + tight tapes
The lesson in one line: understand the anatomy, test tube patency, recognise when gas is going somewhere it should not, and use whichever airway route can actually oxygenate the child.
